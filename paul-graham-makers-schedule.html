Paul Graham reading notes · Commentary

Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule

Two ways of organising time that quietly conflict: one built from short meetings and decisions, the other from long uninterrupted blocks.

An independent interpretation written from my own compiled archive, not a reproduction of the original essay.

01

Two ways to hold a day

A manager's day is built from slots. Meetings fit into them, and a free slot is simply available. A maker's day is built from stretches — programming, writing and research need a long run at one problem.

The conflict is not that meetings take time. It is that a single short meeting in the middle of an afternoon does not remove one hour; it removes the afternoon, by cutting it into two pieces too small to do deep work in.

02

Why interruptions cost more than their length

Hard engineering work runs on a mental model: the code, the assumptions, the constraints, and the half-formed idea you were testing. That model is expensive to build and is not saved anywhere. An interruption discards it, and the real cost is rebuilding it afterwards.

Claims about exactly how many minutes are lost are illustrative rather than measured. The direction is well supported; the precise number is not something I would quote as evidence.

03

A small experiment

Rather than taking the claim on trust, test it on your own week.

  1. Reserve one uninterrupted block for the hardest task you have.
  2. Note what you finished, and how long it took to get going.
  3. Repeat on a day broken by a meeting in the middle.
  4. Compare the two. Your own numbers beat anyone's anecdote.

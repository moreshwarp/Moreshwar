01

Systems and reliability

Two results sit behind most “why is it slow?” questions. Both are easier to feel than to read. The models below run in your browser, with the formula stated so you can check the arithmetic.

Why a server at 90% busy feels broken

model · M/M/1 queue

A road at half capacity flows. The same road at 95% capacity crawls, with no crash and no roadworks. Servers behave the same way. Waiting time does not rise in a straight line as a machine gets busier — it bends upward, then runs away.

Waiting in queue— ms
Total time seen by user— ms
Slower than an idle server—×

Push the first slider past 90%. The work per request never changed — only how often the server was already busy when the next request arrived. This is why capacity planning targets roughly 70%, and why “the CPU is only at 85%” is not reassuring.

What this cannot show — it assumes requests arrive at random and take exponentially distributed time, one server, no priorities. Real systems have bursts, retries and several queues in a row, all of which make it worse rather than better.

Why one rare slow call becomes everyone's problem

model · tail amplification

Suppose a single service is slow only 1% of the time. That sounds harmless. But one page usually needs many services at once, and the page is not ready until the slowest one answers. The rare event stops being rare once you need many answers.

Page waits for a slow call—%
Page is entirely fast—%

With 100 calls and a 1-in-100 chance each, the page is slow about 63% of the time. The formula is 1 − (1 − p)n. Nothing is broken, and almost every user waits anyway. This is why teams track the 99th percentile rather than the average.

What this cannot show — it assumes the calls are independent and all must finish. Shared causes, such as one overloaded database behind all of them, make real systems correlate and perform worse than this.

02

From a question to a test

A plausible explanation is not evidence. This is the sequence I try to hold myself to, and the reason each experiment on this site states its limits beside its result.

  1. State the question, and the competing explanations that would also fit what I have seen.
  2. Decide what can actually be measured, and what would have to be assumed instead.
  3. Pick a baseline. A number with nothing to compare it against is not a finding.
  4. Build the smallest experiment that could separate the explanations, and make it repeatable.
  5. Report what the result cannot tell us, in the same place as the result.

The last step is the one that is usually skipped, and it is the one that decides whether the work is research or decoration.

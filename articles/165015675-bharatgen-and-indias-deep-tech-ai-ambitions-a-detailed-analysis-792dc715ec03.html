BharatGen and India’s Deep Tech AI Ambitions: A Detailed Analysis

The inception of BharatGen represents a pivotal moment in India’s pursuit of prominence within the global artificial intelligence (AI)…

Moreshwar2025-03-064 min read

The inception of BharatGen represents a pivotal moment in India’s pursuit of prominence within the global artificial intelligence (AI) landscape, particularly addressing the intricate challenges associated with linguistic plurality, cultural specificity, and data sovereignty.

Nonetheless, despite the laudable ambitions articulated by this initiative, India’s pathway to global AI leadership encounters substantial barriers. This document provides an exhaustive critical evaluation of India’s infrastructural, educational, regulatory, economic, and technological readiness in AI, juxtaposing these dimensions against comparable efforts in the United States and China.

Infrastructure and Computational Resources

India’s primary bottleneck in deep-tech AI lies in computational infrastructure. AI models like GPT-4, developed by OpenAI, require vast computational power, often demanding tens of thousands of GPUs running simultaneously.

India’s largest supercomputers, such as Param Siddhi-AI, though powerful domestically, rank significantly lower globally compared to the USA’s Summit or China’s Sunway TaihuLight.

For instance:

USA: OpenAI’s partnership with Microsoft leverages Azure’s vast GPU clusters, with investments in infrastructure exceeding $1 billion annually.

China: Alibaba Cloud and Tencent Cloud offer substantial GPU resources to domestic companies, supporting rapid AI iterations and developments.

India: IIT Bombay and other IITs, despite significant efforts, currently possess relatively limited GPU clusters, typically numbering in the hundreds rather than thousands, severely constraining the scale of AI training and development.

Investment in Fundamental AI Research

Fundamental AI research fuels global AI advancements. In the US, fundamental breakthroughs like transformer architectures emerged from academia-industry collaborations at Google Brain and OpenAI. China invests aggressively through institutions like Baidu and universities such as Tsinghua and Peking, focusing heavily on fundamental AI innovations like the ERNIE and WuDao models.

In sharp contrast, India’s approach remains predominantly application-driven, often characterized by incremental enhancements rather than substantial theoretical breakthroughs. According to the Global AI Talent Report (2024), India’s contribution to foundational AI literature remains under 5%, starkly overshadowed by the combined contributions of the USA and China, amounting to nearly 70%.

Talent Retention and Education

The United States continues to attract global talent through world-class institutions like MIT, Stanford, and Carnegie Mellon, hosting nearly 60% of top-tier AI researchers. China aggressively retains talent by offering competitive salaries, modern facilities, and significant research autonomy.

Conversely, India struggles markedly with talent attrition:

  • Approximately 35% of India’s STEM graduates emigrate annually, primarily to the United States and Europe.

  • AI researcher compensation within India frequently lags behind global benchmarks, with remuneration typically three to five times lower than equivalent positions in the United States.

Regulatory and Bureaucratic Environment

The USA has cultivated a conducive regulatory environment, with agile frameworks allowing rapid experimentation and deployment. China, while more centralized, expedites decisions crucial for fast-paced AI research and development.

In India, however, regulatory procedures are notoriously protracted and multilayered, impeding swift technological implementation. Approvals for high-performance computational infrastructure or novel AI deployments routinely extend over months, contrasting sharply with the rapid response timelines typical in China and the United States.

Private Sector Involvement

In the US and China, private companies actively lead AI R&D. Google’s DeepMind, OpenAI, Tencent AI Lab, and Alibaba’s Damo Academy demonstrate strong private sector investment in deep-tech research.

India’s private sector remains relatively risk-averse:

Major Indian corporations like Tata, Reliance, and Infosys predominantly adopt existing AI technologies rather than invest substantially in long-term R&D.

Venture capital investment in Indian AI startups was approximately $2 billion in 2023, compared to $25 billion in the US and nearly $15 billion in China.

Risk Capital and Venture Funding

Risk appetite significantly differentiates India’s ecosystem from its global counterparts. The US venture capital ecosystem consistently funds ambitious, long-term AI projects (e.g., OpenAI’s multi-billion-dollar funding rounds). China similarly fosters robust venture funding, especially in early-stage deep-tech startups.

In contrast, India’s investors predominantly fund proven business models rather than high-risk, high-reward AI innovations. According to NASSCOM, fewer than 10% of Indian AI startups receive funding beyond Series A, severely limiting the scale and ambition of domestic innovation.

Global vs. Local Vision

AI innovation in the US and China emphasizes global applications — autonomous vehicles, global healthcare solutions, and scalable digital services. India’s focus has primarily been domestic, centering around local languages, e-commerce assistance, and data sovereignty.

While addressing local needs is critical, limiting ambition to domestic contexts misses opportunities to lead globally transformative AI solutions.

Learning and Recommendations

India must adopt several critical strategies:
Massive Infrastructure Investment: Create AI-specific national infrastructure through extensive public-private partnerships.
Promote Fundamental Research: Shift investment towards long-term foundational research.
Reverse Brain Drain: Offer competitive compensation, world-class facilities, and research freedom.
Regulatory Reforms: Streamline bureaucratic processes to accelerate AI innovation.
Private Sector Activation: Incentivize significant private-sector investment in AI R&D.
Encourage Risk Capital: Develop policies fostering a robust venture ecosystem willing to back ambitious, high-risk AI startups.

Hope for the Future

Despite significant challenges, BharatGen exemplifies India’s potential and ambition in the global AI arena. By addressing critical shortcomings through strategic international benchmarking and proactive implementation of best practices, India is well-positioned to transition from aspirational AI participant to global technology leader.

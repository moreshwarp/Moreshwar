The Secret Link Between “Sounding Good” and “Being Right”

Based on the essay "Good Writing" by Paul Graham.

Moreshwar2026-01-075 min read

We often think of writing as having two separate scorecards.

  1. Style: Does it sound good? Are the sentences flowing and pleasant?

  2. Substance: Is it true? Are the ideas logical and correct?

To most of us, these seem like completely different variables, like the speed of a car versus the color of its paint. You can have a fast ugly car, or a slow beautiful one. But Paul Graham argues something that sounds almost impossible: in writing, the paint actually makes the car go faster.

Graham suggests that writing that sounds good is significantly more likely to be right.1 Style and substance aren’t enemies; they are partners.

The “Preposterous” Truth

Common wisdom says you can’t optimize two unrelated things at the same time. If you focus too much on making sentences pretty, you usually sacrifice depth.

But Graham’s experience contradicts this. He found that when he paused to fix a sentence that sounded clunky or awkward, he wasn’t just fixing the grammar—he was often fixing the idea itself.

The Core Insight: If a sentence is hard to write or hard to read, it usually means the underlying thought is muddy. Fixing the “sound” forces you to clarify the “thought.”

Mechanism 1: The “Shaking the Bin” Effect

How does this work? Graham offers a fascinating real-life example from his early days in typesetting.

Sometimes, a paragraph would be just one line too long to fit on a page. To fix the layout, he had to rewrite the paragraph to make it slightly shorter. You would think that forcing an arbitrary length limit would hurt the quality of the writing.

Surprisingly, the opposite happened. The shorter, constrained version was almost always better than the original.

The Real-Life Analogy: The Jar of Nuts

Imagine you are trying to fit mixed nuts into a jar, but they are overflowing. You shake the jar. The shaking is random—you aren’t placing each nut individually. Yet, gravity and vibration cause the nuts to settle, packing themselves tighter and more efficiently.

Writing works the same way:

  • The Jar: Your essay.

  • The Nuts: Your ideas and words.

  • The Shake: The act of rewriting to fix an awkward sentence or meet a length constraint.

When you are forced to rewrite a clunky sentence (shaking the jar), you strip away the fluff. You can’t make the idea less true (gravity prevents that), so the only direction you can move is toward clarity. The “arbitrary” act of polishing the sound inevitably tightens the logic.

Mechanism 2: The Writer as the First Reader

There is a practical reason why “sounding good” helps you get to the truth: Friction.

Writing isn’t just typing; it is mostly reading. Graham notes that for every minute he spends writing, he spends much more time re-reading what he just wrote. He compares this to sanding a piece of wood. You run your hand over the wood (read the text) to see if there are any splinters or rough patches (awkward phrasing).

  • If the writing flows poorly, your brain has to work hard just to process the sentences. The “noise” of bad writing hides the logic errors.

  • If the writing flows smoothly, the logic is laid bare. If there is a gap in your thinking, it becomes immediately obvious because there is no bad grammar to hide behind.

The Analogy: Driving on a Highway

  • Bad Writing: Driving on a pothole-filled road. You are so focused on dodging bumps that you don’t notice if the engine is making a weird noise.

  • Good Writing: Driving on a perfectly smooth highway. Because the ride is silent, you immediately hear the faint rattle of a loose engine part (a flaw in your logic).

Mechanism 3: Rhythm is the Shape of Thought

When we say writing “sounds good,” we usually mean it has good rhythm. But this isn’t the repetitive rhythm of a nursery rhyme. It is the complex, organic rhythm of a conversation.

An essay is essentially a “cleaned-up train of thought.” Thoughts have shapes. Some are short and punchy; others are long and winding.

  • If your sentence rhythm doesn’t match the shape of the idea, it sounds “off.”

  • When a writer thinks, “Ugh, this sentence sounds terrible,” what they are usually detecting is, “I don’t actually know what I’m trying to say here.”

By fixing the rhythm, you force yourself to align the words with the true shape of the idea.

The Skeptic’s Question: What About Liars?

If beautiful writing equals truth, does that mean a smooth-talking con artist is telling the truth?

Not exactly. A liar can write beautifully, but they have to use Method Acting. To write a smooth, flowing essay that is false, the liar has to construct a fake reality where their lie would be true, and then write consistently within that bubble.

So, Graham refines his rule:

  • Good writing doesn’t guarantee truth, but it guarantees internal consistency.

  • Bad writing, however, is a strong signal of bad thinking.

If you read something that is clumsy, full of jargon, and hard to follow, it is rarely because the ideas are “too complex” for you. It is usually because the writer hasn’t fully figured out the ideas themselves.

Conclusion: The Rope

Style and Substance are not two separate ends of a stick. They are more like a rope made of many overlapping strands. You cannot twist one end of the rope (style) without the other end (substance) moving as well.

If you are a researcher, a student, or a professional, do not treat editing as “polishing.”
Treat it as “thinking.” When you make your writing sound better, you are not just painting
the car; you are tuning the engine.

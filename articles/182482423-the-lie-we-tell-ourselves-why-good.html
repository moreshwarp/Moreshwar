The Lie We Tell Ourselves: Why "Good" Isn't Just an Opinion

For a long time, I believed something that comforted me. Whenever I saw a piece of art I didn’t understand, or a design that looked weird, I would shrug and say, "Well, beauty is in the eye of the beholder." It’s a nice phrase. It’s polite. It means nobody is wrong, and nobody has to feel bad about their work.

But recently, I realized that this idea is a trap. It’s a fog that stops us from seeing the truth. If everything is subjective—if my scribbles are equal to a master’s painting just because "I like them"—then there is no such thing as getting better. And if there’s no such thing as getting better, then why am I working so hard?

The truth is, "Good" is not just a feeling. It is real. It is something we can find, measure, and build.

The Difference Between "I Like" and "It Is"

To understand this, I had to learn the difference between being subjective and being objective.

Subjective is what happens inside my head. If I walk into a room and say, "It feels cold," that’s my opinion. You might feel warm. We can argue about it all day, and neither of us is wrong.

Objective is what happens in the real world. If I look at a thermometer and it says 18°C, that is a fact. It doesn't care how I feel.

For years, I thought design and art were like the temperature—totally up to how I felt. But Paul Graham taught me that good work is actually like the thermometer. It’s not about what the creator "likes." It’s about whether the creation works.

The Human Machine

Here is the biggest realization: Humans are not as different as we pretend to be. We all share 99.9% of the same DNA. We are built from the same blueprint.

Because we have the same hardware (eyes, brains, nervous systems), we react to things in the same way. We all find a symmetrical face more trustworthy than a crooked one. We all find a clear, simple image easier to understand than a messy one.

This means that "Good Design" isn’t magic. It’s just a solution that fits perfectly with the human machine. If I build a website that confuses people, I can’t say, "You just don't get my style." No, the design is objectively bad because it failed to communicate with the human brain. It’s like a key that doesn’t fit the lock. You can't argue with the lock.

The Mountain and the Fog

So why do we disagree on what is good? Why do I like one thing and an expert likes another?

Imagine a giant mountain. At the very top is "Perfection"—the absolute best way to do something. But the bottom of the mountain is covered in thick fog.

When we are beginners, we are standing at the bottom in the fog. We can’t see the peak. We just see the blurry shapes around us. We pick things that look flashy or cool because we don't know any better. We think, "This is great!" because we can't see what lies above us.

But the experts—the top 1%—have climbed out of the fog. They can see the peak. And the amazing thing is, the higher people climb, the more they agree with each other. The best architects, the best coders, and the best writers all tend to value the same things: simplicity, clarity, and strength. They aren't guessing anymore. They are looking at the same truth.

Fashion vs. The Timeless

There is one last trap: Fashion.

Sometimes we mistake "new" for "good." We look at a trendy gadget or a popular coding style and think it’s amazing just because everyone else has it. But that’s not quality; that’s just social pressure.

The real test is time. A simple wooden chair made 200 years ago is still useful and beautiful today. It is "Good." A neon inflatable chair from the 1990s looks ridiculous now. It was just "Fashion."

My Conclusion

I don’t want to be stuck in the fog, arguing about taste. I want to climb the mountain. I want to build things—software, systems, or even just ideas—that work perfectly.

To do that, I have to stop asking, "Do I like this?" and start asking, "Is this true? Does this work? Is this simple?"

"Good" is out there, waiting for us to find it. We just have to be humble enough to admit that the mountain is real, and start climbing.
